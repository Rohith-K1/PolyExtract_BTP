How long are the chains, and what does that do to the material?

This is a report on my project: a dataset that links the molecular-weight numbers polymer papers report (Mn, Mw, Đ and a few others) to properties measured on the same samples, with the table row or sentence behind every value. It is not finished. Below is what exists today, what went wrong on the way, and what I plan to do next.

The honest reading: the pipeline works end to end and the first release is out, but I am roughly a quarter of the way to the original target. Most of the gap is a parser problem I now understand, not a shortage of papers.

In plain words

A record is one row: one polymer sample, its molecular-weight numbers, and one measured property, with the place in the paper it came from.

617 / 109 means 617 distinct samples from 109 papers.

Why this data is worth collecting

A polymer is not one molecule. It is millions of chains of different lengths. The average length (Mn, Mw, Mz) and how widely the lengths vary (Đ) shift the glass transition, the viscosity, the strength and how the material can be processed.

Papers do report these numbers, but each in its own units and table layout, sometimes only in a sentence of the experimental section. To compare them you currently read the papers one by one. I am putting them in a single table where every value keeps its source next to it. Once that table is large and clean enough, three things become possible:

  • Predict. Give a sample's Mn and Đ and get an estimate of its Tg or viscosity.
  • Find. Ask for every sample with Mw under 50,000 g/mol and Tg above 100 °C, and get the list with sources.
  • Answer with evidence. Ask a question in plain English and get an answer that quotes the sentence it came from.

Early versions of all three are in the review app. They use only human-checked records, and there are not enough of those yet for the results to mean much.

In plain words

Mn counts every chain once. Mw gives long chains more weight, and Mz even more. That is why Mn < Mw < Mz for any real sample.

DP is the same information counted in repeat units instead of g/mol. It is common for cellulose, where papers rarely give Mn.

How the dataset is built

Five stages. Nothing here is generated from scratch: a number enters the dataset only if it can be found in the paper.

  1. Find the papers

    Search the OpenAlex index with polymer-specific queries, then filter for relevance.

    so far 3,198 papers

  2. Get the full text, legally

    Only papers with an open licence. Paywalled papers are logged and skipped. I do not scrape publisher sites.

    so far 1,101 usable · 33 paywalled · 34 open PDFs not parsed yet · 121 unclear

  3. Read every paper twice

    A rule-based reader pulls numbers out of the tables. Separately, an AI model reads the relevant tables and passages. Each value must appear word for word in the paper, or it is thrown away. A record counts as double-found only when both readers agree.

    so far AI reader has covered 504 papers; about 600 downloaded papers are still waiting

  4. Run automatic checks

    Five groups of checks: data format, evidence match, unit conversion, physical plausibility, and internal consistency (for example Mw / Mn must match the reported Đ). A record that fails is blocked and cannot be accepted.

  5. A person checks

    I compare each table or passage with the original and then accept, reject, or send it back for re-extraction. Every decision is logged with the exact values I saw.

    so far 1,045 records decided by hand

In plain words

Open-access XML. The structured version of an article. Tables arrive as real rows and columns instead of a picture, which is why these papers are far easier to read automatically.

AI reader. A language model used only as a reading tool. It can point at a number in the text. It is not allowed to supply one.

What went wrong along the way

Reviewing by hand is where I learned the most. Of the 1,045 records I decided on, about two in three were fine as extracted. The rest were not.

  • 698 accepted
  • 192 rejected
  • 155 sent back for re-extraction

“Rejected” means the numbers were copied correctly but should not be in the dataset. “Sent back” means the paper has good data and my extraction mangled it. Here are the six kinds of trouble, with a real example of each.

  1. layout

    Two numbers in one cell

    Some tables write Mn and Đ together, like “121.6 [1.09]”. The first parser read that as a single number, so a dispersity of 121.6 appeared. The plausibility check blocked those rows, and I sent the tables back.

  2. units

    A missing power of ten

    Headers like “Mw × 10⁻³” or “MW (10⁶)” carry the scale. With it dropped, a 22,000 g/mol polymer became 22 g/mol. In one table the superscript was lost and 10⁵ simply read “105”. The range check caught most of these. A value of 70 g/mol slipped through and I only noticed it by looking at the table.

  3. wrong column

    Columns that look right and are not

    A column called “E′ at Tg − 20 °C” was read as the glass transition, and so was “Tg breadth”. In another table the header changed halfway down, so crystallisation and melting temperatures of the PEG rows became Tg values. The abbreviation PSU meant polysulfone to the software and a PDMS-based polyurea to the authors.

  4. not a measurement

    True numbers that do not belong

    A value can be copied perfectly and still be unusable: a density from the supplier’s datasheet, a Tg from a molecular-dynamics simulation, a molecular weight calculated from the very viscosity it is meant to explain, a clay nanocomposite, a crosslinked network, a gel with no stated concentration. I wrote rules for these and rejected them even when the extraction was flawless.

  5. AI error

    The AI fills gaps

    When a paper gave only Mn, the AI reader once copied it into Mw as well. That produced Mw equal to Mn with a dispersity of 1.17, which cannot be true. It is the main reason a record needs a second reader, a word-for-word evidence match and a person, and why I want a stricter Mw / Mn check.

  6. access

    Only what is legally open

    I do not bypass paywalls. Of 1,318 papers checked for full text, 1,101 were usable. The cost is bias: 79% of today’s records come from MDPI journals, which publish open access, and 60% from one of them (Polymers).

Why this matters for the result

An automatic pipeline alone would have put several hundred wrong or misleading rows into the dataset, and each one would have looked confident. The checks and the review are part of the method, not a clean-up step.

How much to trust it

The 1,086 records are not all equal, and the dataset labels which is which.

To find out how reliable the second tier is, I check a random sample of it. 21 of 100 sampled records are done so far, and 18 were correct.

green correct · red wrong · empty not checked yet

18 of 21 is 86%, but with so few checked the true figure could sit anywhere from about 65% to 95%. I will not count the second tier as verified until at least 100 records are audited and the lower end of that range reaches 90%. The three wrong ones were all about material scope (a grafted nanoparticle, a polymer blend, and one theoretical value), not wrong numbers.

Known gaps

  • Half of the records (49%) are glass-transition temperatures. Other properties are thin.
  • 57% of records still carry the paper’s own sample name instead of a standard polymer family.
  • Coverage is open-access only, and mostly MDPI (79%), with papers from 2013 to 2026.
  • DPz, skewness and kurtosis are empty.
  • I have not trained any prediction model yet. The data is not ready for that to mean anything.
In plain words

Audit. Checking a random sample to estimate how often the machine is right, the way a quality inspector tests a batch instead of every item.

The range 65 to 95%. A small sample cannot pin an accuracy down exactly. This range is how the uncertainty is stated honestly.

Where it is going

  1. Next

    Fix what the review exposed

    Teach the parser to split “Mn [Đ]” cells, keep scale factors, ignore Tg look-alike columns, read the polymer class from the paper’s title, and capture test conditions such as temperature, frequency and draw ratio. Then re-run the roughly 640 records now waiting. I do not yet know how many will survive review, and I will report the real number after the re-run.

    Finish the audit (79 of 100 still to check) and keep reading the downloaded papers that have not been read yet.

  2. Then

    Widen the coverage

    More search queries across other polymer families and properties, and the 34 open-access PDFs the pipeline cannot yet parse. Add standard polymer-family names to the records that lack them.

  3. After that

    Use it

    First baseline models, for example Tg against Mn and Đ within one polymer family, and a proper test of the find and answer tools on verified data.

End goal. An openly documented dataset of a few thousand verified records (the original aim was about 4,600), where every value can be traced to the sentence or table row it came from, with the predict, find and answer tools built on top of it.

A note on the target

The aim stays at about 4,600 verified records. What I can promise today is the method, and a number that is checked rather than inflated.

What I would like from you

  • Scope rules. I exclude composites, blends, crosslinked or filled samples, supplier datasheet values, simulations, and values calculated from the property itself. You will know better than I do where those lines should sit. Please tell me if any should change.
  • Priorities. Tg is half the dataset. Which properties or polymer families would be most useful to deepen first?
  • A spot check, if you have the time. Ten records picked at random, checked against their papers. Even that small outside check would tell me more than my own review can.
